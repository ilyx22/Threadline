## 1. How the session runs (60 minutes, hard stop)

**Method under test** (DME §(d), L180–202): the hook is on the teleprompter, never the body. The body is argued from bullet beats in the owner's own words. Two or three hook variants per angle. At most 2 retakes, and only of the opener, only when the first 3 seconds are weak. Open storytelling at the end. If the owner freezes, the producer asks a question and the angle drops to interview capture instead of failing (DME L200).

**Producer.** Ideally a second person who reads the interview questions and calls retakes. If the owner is alone, he self-directs: after each angle, watch back only the first 3 seconds, then decide on a retake. Note "solo" in the log, because it weakens the conversation arm.

**Kit.** The owner records on his own device (in-browser capture is missing by design, stack inventory §4). Teleprompter: the Recording Room on `test-dry-run` if the hooks are loaded there as scripts, which also exercises T2. Otherwise use any teleprompter app and note which. Beats go on a paper card or a second screen, never on the prompter.

| Clock | Block | What happens | Log |
|---|---|---|---|
| 00–05 | Setup | Framing, sound, a 10-second test clip played back. Prompter speed set on hook 1a. | Device, prompter used, solo or with producer |
| 05–15 | **Arm B: pure conversation (10 min)** | The producer asks the §3 questions in order. No prompter, no beats. The owner answers freely. Follow-ups only: "Give me an example." "What happened next?" | Start/end time; questions reached |
| 15–51 | **Arm A: directed session (about 36 min)** | Angles in the owner's scoring order (§5, highest "Y" first). Per angle, about 3 min: read hooks a, b and c from the prompter, each followed by one second of silence. Pick the best one by feel, then argue the body from the beats (45–90 s). Retake the opener only if the first 3 s are weak (cap 2). | Per angle: start time, hook used, retakes, body length |
| 51–56 | Open story (5 min) | One producer question, Kalungi/Sway style (DME L162, L191): "Tell me about a time a piece of content you worked on failed. What was the problem, what did you try first, and what fixed it?" No client or employer names. | Start/end time |
| 56–60 | Close | Stop recording. Fill in the session log (§5) while it's fresh. | Total session minutes |

**Why the conversation arm goes first.** Recording it after 36 minutes of directed angles would prime the answers with the beats and leave the shorter arm with a tired speaker. Going first, the owner hasn't rehearsed the angles yet, so it's the cleaner baseline. The cost is that the directed arm benefits from the warm-up. Note this in C01.

**Overrun rule.** At 51:00, stop the directed arm wherever it is. The open story still runs. Angles not reached go to a later session; that doesn't count against T1, which scores the brief, not the session.

**What C01 compares** (DME L192–197): active edit minutes per accepted clip; share of clips usable without restructuring; the owner's blind rating of the first 3 seconds; session minutes including prep. Run the same R1–R3 editing routes on both arms.

## 2. The twelve angles

Hook labels follow the DME §(c) 9 taxonomy (L171–173): **bold** claim, **callout**, **question**, **curiosity+benefit**, **number**. Word counts are given as (n). Beats are prompts for the owner, not lines to read.

### A1 · Judge a post against your own normal, not likes
- **Belief:** a post's reactions mean nothing until you compare them with your own usual numbers.
- **Why the buyer cares:** AI-advisory founders who post can't tell a strong post from a big-audience day. The measurement/learning-loop gap is scored on 18 of 59 firms, but can't be seen from outside, so it is pure hypothesis (E21 L59). Method: RP L9–14.
- **Hooks:**
  - a (number): "400 likes can be a bad post. 90 can be a great one." (13)
  - b (callout): "AI consultants: stop judging your posts by likes." (8)
  - c (question): "Was your last post good, or just seen by more people?" (11)
- **Beats:**
  1. A big account's weak post beats a small account's strong one on raw likes. That misleads you.
  2. Fix: take your last 10 posts, find the median, divide each post by it.
  3. That's how we scored 43 posts from 15 AI consultants. Small sample, directional.
  4. Then look only at the posts above 2× and ask what they share.
- **Proof question for the owner:** "In your own ad or content work, did you ever judge something against its own baseline instead of the raw number? What decision did it change?"
- **Pattern:** DME §(c) 1 (outliers against each account's own average); RP method.
- **Risk:** hook a's numbers are illustrative (PBC E7, as in R9 LI-B1). Label them "illustrative" if shown on screen. Keep the framing about *how to read* a post, not "metrics don't matter", which stays clear of the D04 views-scepticism family (H01 §3.1).

### A2 · A scene from real work beats any thesis
- **Belief:** for a consultant, one scene from real work does more than any argument about AI.
- **Why the buyer cares:** it was the pattern most native to consultants in the pilot (RP L21–25), and abstract thesis posts with no scene or number underperformed (RP L53).
- **Hooks:**
  - a (bold): "The posts that win for consultants start in a client's room." (11)
  - b (curiosity+benefit): "Your best post is the request you turned down last month." (11)
  - c (callout): "Stop explaining AI strategy. Tell me what happened in the meeting." (11)
- **Beats:**
  1. In our sample, posts that opened on a scene (a request turned down, a room of people) ran about 2.5–3× their authors' normal.
  2. Scene first, lesson second. Never the other way round.
  3. Client unnamed, details changed, nothing identifiable.
  4. End on a real question you'd want answered, not "comment YES" (RP L32).
- **Proof question for the owner:** "Is there a moment from your own work (no names, no employer) where one scene explained more than a deck could? Can you tell it in 20 seconds without identifying anyone?"
- **Pattern:** RP pattern 1; DME §(c) 5 (expertise extraction by question).
- **Risk:** 2.5–3× is other creators' posts in our sample, never Threadline's result. The owner's own scene must not identify a client or employer.

### A3 · Reach from builders is the wrong reach
- **Belief:** an AI consultant who grows an audience of other builders has grown the wrong audience.
- **Why the buyer cares:** the biggest YouTube multiples came from builder-facing channels whose audience doesn't buy consulting. The buyer-attracting angles were case studies, one job function, and teardowns (RP L70–76).
- **Hooks:**
  - a (bold): "The most-watched AI videos are the wrong ones for consultants to copy." (12)
  - b (bold): "Builders watch your videos. Builders don't hire consultants." (8)
  - c (curiosity+benefit): "Your AI videos are popular with exactly the people who won't buy." (12)
- **Beats:**
  1. In our sample, 6–10× jumps came from "I tested this tool" and fast news reactions.
  2. Those channels serve builders. Builders comment; they don't book advisory calls.
  3. What drew buyers looked quieter: one firm's case, one job done differently, a teardown.
  4. Before posting, ask: would a buyer of consulting stop for this?
- **Proof question for the owner:** "Have you seen an account grow an audience that never bought? What told you the audience was wrong?"
- **Pattern:** DME §(c) 2 (expert filters: judge against a narrow audience, skip entertainment outliers).
- **Risk:** **D04 HOLD for posting.** It sits in the "wrong audience / views are weak evidence" family that H01 holds until D04 closes (H01 §3.1, L131–135). Recording is fine. The YouTube figures are third-party scrapes (RP L84).

### A4 · Webinar posts lose the audience
- **Belief:** an event promo is the weakest thing a consultant can post. Post the idea from the event instead.
- **Why the buyer cares:** advisory firms run webinars as lead generation. In the pilot, webinar and registration promos were among the clearest underperformers (RP L48–55), and promotion worked best after value, as a P.S. or first comment (RP L45–46).
- **Hooks:**
  - a (bold): "Webinar posts are where good AI consultants lose their audience." (10)
  - b (number): "A million followers. Under thirty reactions. It was a webinar post." (10)
  - c (callout): "Stop announcing your webinar. Post the one idea from it instead." (11)
- **Beats:**
  1. In our sample, promos from an account with about a million followers drew 2 to 28 reactions each.
  2. A promo asks for a commitment before giving anything.
  3. Instead, post the sharpest single point from the session as its own piece.
  4. The registration link goes in a P.S. or the first comment.
- **Proof question for the owner:** "Have you promoted an event yourself? How did the promo do compared with content cut from the event?"
- **Pattern:** DME §(c) 7 (the "would the ICP share it?" test).
- **Risk:** one account, so never name it, and say "in our sample". Hook b compresses "2–28" to "under thirty", which is accurate but must not become "under ten".

### A5 · Your positioning line is on your competitor's site
- **Belief:** in AI advisory the positioning lines have converged, so visible proof of work is what's left to tell firms apart.
- **Why the buyer cares:** differentiation and commoditisation pressure is the desk's top-ranked problem (H2, high confidence, not buyer-validated; E21 L70). Converging lines were found across firms' own sites (E21 L40–41).
- **Hooks:**
  - a (number): "Seven firms we reviewed promise not to hand you a report and leave." (13)
  - b (bold): "If your positioning line is on a competitor's site, it isn't positioning." (12)
  - c (curiosity+benefit): "AI consultancies all sound the same now. Here's what still separates them." (12)
- **Beats:**
  1. In our review of 59 firms, 7 said a version of "we don't hand you a report and leave", and 6 led with "people before technology".
  2. That isn't a criticism. The doctrine is right, so everyone says it.
  3. What a competitor can't copy is a buyer watching you work through a real problem.
  4. So show the work: one decision, one scene, one number you can source.
- **Proof question for the owner:** "When you wrote Threadline's own positioning, did you catch yourself writing a line competitors already use? What did you replace it with?"
- **Pattern:** DME §(c) 6 (positioning before ideas); RP pattern 3 (a field observation backed by one fact).
- **Risk:** the counts are our desk coding of public pages captured 26 Sept. Re-verify before posting. Never name or quote a firm. The buyer-pain link is hypothesis.

### A6 · A framework nobody has seen you use is just a claim
- **Belief:** a named framework on your site is a claim; one video of you using it is proof.
- **Why the buyer cares:** proof of work is the most common gap scored across the 59 firms (35 rows, E21 L53). 9 firms show named frameworks (E21 L45), and several had a newest article years old (E21 L64). The desk reading is that the doctrine exists and the gap is proof and distribution.
- **Hooks:**
  - a (question): "Your framework has a trademark. Has anyone ever seen you use it?" (12)
  - b (number): "Proof of work is the gap we found most often in AI advisory firms." (14)
  - c (callout): "Stop describing your framework. Walk one decision through it on camera." (11)
- **Beats:**
  1. A framework diagram is something a buyer skims.
  2. One real decision walked through one step (client anonymised) shows judgement.
  3. You can film it in one sitting from what you already know.
  4. Pick one step of your framework and give it 60 seconds.
- **Proof question for the owner:** "Could you walk Threadline's own four-check gate (authority, curiosity, perceived outcome, ICP relevance; PBC B-11, usable) through one of today's hooks, live?"
- **Pattern:** DME §(c) 2 (filmable in one sitting from existing knowledge; keep the profession's own terms).
- **Risk:** the gap counts are our desk scoring, so treat them as hypothesis. Don't say "almost none show it", because nothing measured that. Name no firm.

### A7 · Your sales calls are your content backlog
- **Belief:** the best content for an advisory firm has already been said, on its sales calls.
- **Why the buyer cares:** making expertise visible before the sales call is a desk-high hypothesis (H3, E21 L71). It's also the approved thesis of S-4, X-4 and TH-4 in the existing inventory (H01 L41, L76, L115).
- **Hooks:**
  - a (callout): "Stop brainstorming content. Open last week's sales-call notes." (8)
  - b (curiosity+benefit): "The answer you give on every second sales call is your next video." (13)
  - c (bold): "Your best content idea was a buyer's objection. You answered it, then forgot it." (14)
- **Beats:**
  1. The questions buyers ask before buying are the ones your content should answer before the call.
  2. After each call, write down the objection and the answer you gave.
  3. Record that answer in 60 seconds, in your own words.
  4. Tag it to one root idea so you learn which argument works, not which format (PBC B-12, usable).
- **Proof question for the owner:** "From your own past sales work, which question did buyers ask again and again? Use it only if it's real; skip it if not."
- **Pattern:** DME §(c) 5 (expertise extraction by question).
- **Risk:** H3 has no buyer voice yet (E21 L32). Any notes shown on screen must be labelled demo notes, never real (H01 L115).

### A8 · One job function beats AI news
- **Belief:** for a consultant, AI applied to one job the buyer does beats any AI headline.
- **Why the buyer cares:** in the pilot, "AI applied to one job function" beat general AI news (RP L35–36), and one-line news reshares underperformed (RP L50). It's one of the three buyer-attracting patterns (RP L73–74).
- **Hooks:**
  - a (bold): "Nobody hires a consultant for AI news. They hire for their finance team." (13)
  - b (callout): "Stop posting model launches. Show one job done differently." (9)
  - c (callout): "Pick one job your buyer does. Make every AI post about that job." (13)
- **Beats:**
  1. In our sample, posts on one job function beat general AI news; finance creators showed 5–6×.
  2. A buyer recognises their own Tuesday faster than a model release.
  3. Format: "you do X; the teams ahead of you do Y."
  4. Choose one function and stay with it long enough to learn from the replies.
- **Proof question for the owner:** "In your own work, did narrowing to one audience's actual job ever change the response? What happened?"
- **Pattern:** RP pattern 5; DME §(c) 2 (a narrow audience, not general views).
- **Risk:** 5–6× is other creators' data from a small sample. Don't present it as a rule.

### A9 · Put a number in the first line, and never invent it
- **Belief:** one real number in the first line does more than three adjectives, and one invented number costs more than it earns.
- **Why the buyer cares:** a hard number in or near the hook was a pattern in both sources (RP L26–28). 15 of 59 firms already lead with hard numbers, all their own unaudited claims (E21 L44), so a buyer's scepticism is the context.
- **Hooks:**
  - a (bold): "Your first line needs a number. Your reputation needs it to be true." (13)
  - b (callout): "Firm size, days, money, a count: put one in your first line." (12)
  - c (question): "A number in your hook is a promise. Can you show the source?" (12)
- **Beats:**
  1. Good numbers: team size, days, hours, a count. One is enough.
  2. Never round up, never invent one.
  3. Keep three kinds apart: measured, client-reported, your own estimate.
  4. If you can't source a number, use a scene instead (see A2).
- **Proof question for the owner:** "Is there a number from your own work you could state and source if a buyer asked? If not, which scene would you use instead? (Not the site's headline figures; those are never-use in content, PBC A-01/A-02.)"
- **Pattern:** RP pattern 2; DME §(c) 9 (bold claim; social proof up front).
- **Risk:** the owner must not reach for A-01/A-02 or any E3 track-record figure. Anything personal he cites is E3 and needs substantiation before posting (PBC L18, L189).

### A10 · Buyers hire judgement, and tips don't show any
- **Belief:** a tip shows you know the topic; a judgement shows you know the buyer's situation, and buyers hire the second.
- **Why the buyer cares:** short, debatable claims drew comments equal to or above reactions, while bare questions and polls with no position flopped (RP L43–44). The approved rewrites of LI-8 and S-9 make the same point (H01 L102, L120).
- **Hooks:**
  - a (bold): "Anyone can post five AI tips. Buyers hire the person who says which one's wrong." (15)
  - b (bold): "A tip shows you know the topic. A judgement shows you know them." (13)
  - c (bold, worked example): "'Your pilot stalled because nobody owned the decision after it.' That's a judgement." (12)
- **Beats:**
  1. A tip is general and safe: "post consistently."
  2. A judgement names a cause in the buyer's situation, and you can be wrong.
  3. Example (illustrative): reach with no buyer replies may mean the audience is wrong, not the idea.
  4. Take a position, then ask the real question.
- **Proof question for the owner:** "What's one judgement you hold about founder content that most people in your field would disagree with?"
- **Pattern:** RP patterns 3 and 8; DME §(c) 7.
- **Risk:** the hook c line and beat 3 are illustrative (PBC E7), not a client finding. Beat 3 touches the D04 audience family, so keep it as an example of a judgement, not a claim about views.

### A11 · Buyers hire the principal, so the principal should be on camera
- **Belief:** in advisory, buyers are hiring the senior person, so that person, not the company page, should do the talking.
- **Why the buyer cares:** "senior principal, not a bench" recurs in firms' own positioning (3 rows, E21 L42), and the desk finding is that founder authority is the commercial asset (E21 L42, S1 finding B; hypothesis). Video only helps when it matches the post text (RP L62).
- **Hooks:**
  - a (question): "Your clients hire you, not your logo. So why is your logo posting?" (13)
  - b (bold): "If you sell senior judgement, your company page can't deliver it." (11)
  - c (curiosity+benefit): "Thirty seconds of you on camera says what your About page can't." (12)
- **Beats:**
  1. A company page can say "senior". A short video lets a buyer judge it before the call.
  2. You don't need to be a presenter: one sharp point, in your own words.
  3. Script the first line only (see A12); talk the rest the way you do with clients.
  4. Put the same point in the post text, because video works when the two match.
- **Proof question for the owner:** "Why are you recording this yourself rather than posting as Threadline? What changed your mind, or what would?"
- **Pattern:** DME §(c) 6 (positioning before ideas); §(c) 5.
- **Risk:** **this argues against Threadline's own current company-led rule** (§0 item 1). The owner should decide that before publishing this one, or it reads as "do as I say". "Thirty seconds" is a clip length, not a founder-time claim; keep it that way.

### A12 · Script the first two seconds, not the argument
- **Belief:** an expert should script the opening line and argue the rest in their own words.
- **Why the buyer cares:** senior experts resist "presenting", and reading a full script sounds robotic without practice (DME L131–133). Practitioners converge on "script the opener, loosen the body" (DME L110–113). This is the method being tested today.
- **Hooks:**
  - a (bold): "Don't script your video. Script the first two seconds." (9)
  - b (callout): "Teleprompter for one line. Then talk like you do with clients." (11)
  - c (bold): "Experts sound worse reading a script. So only script one line." (11)
- **Beats:**
  1. The first seconds decide whether anyone hears your argument.
  2. Ad practice: lock the body, try several openers, keep the best (DME L106–109).
  3. Hook on the prompter, three bullet points on a card, retake only the opener.
  4. Honest close: this video was made exactly that way; we're testing it on ourselves first.
- **Proof question for the owner:** "From your performance-ad work, how many openers would you try against one body, and what did you look at to pick one?"
- **Pattern:** DME §(c) 9 (hook taxonomy); DME §(b) "For" 1–2.
- **Risk:** there's no controlled evidence that this beats interview capture (DME L139, L142). Present it as how we work, not as proven. Quote no third-party hook statistics.

## 3. Interview questions for the conversation arm (10 minutes, no prompter)

Same subject as the angles, so the editing comparison is fair. None of them invites a result, a promise or a guarantee. If an answer drifts into one, the producer moves to the next question.

1. What made you start Threadline? What did you keep seeing expert firms get wrong about content?
2. Walk me through how you'd decide what an AI consultant should say on video this week.
3. What's the mistake you see AI consultants make most often when they post?
4. What does "clipping a podcast" get wrong, and when is it actually fine?
5. If a founder could change only one thing about how they post, what should it be, and why?
6. What did you believe about founder content a year ago that you've since changed your mind on?

Producer follow-ups only: "Give me an example." "What happened next?" "Say that in one sentence."
