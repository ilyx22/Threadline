## LinkedIn Company Page (5)

### LI-B1 · Data/observation · Expertise · MOF · Methodology/competence
We spent a week studying 43 LinkedIn posts from 15 AI consultants.

Not to copy them. To find out what actually makes a consultant's post outperform their own usual
numbers.

So we didn't rank by likes. A post with 400 reactions from someone who normally gets 1,000 is a
weak post. A post with 90 from someone who normally gets 30 is a strong one. Every post was scored
against its author's own median.

Three things kept showing up in the posts that ran well above normal:

1. A scene from real client work, client unnamed. A request turned down. A room full of heads of
AI. Then the lesson.
2. A hard number in or near the first line. Firm size, days, money, a count.
3. An opening that reads like an observation from the field, backed by one fact.

And the things that underperformed were just as consistent: news reshares, link drops, webinar
promos, and big abstract thesis posts with no scene and no number.

The sample is small and directional. But if you advise companies on AI and your posts sit flat,
check the last five against those three. Most flat posts we read had none of them.

### LI-B2 · Contrarian (earned) · Opinion · TOF · Belief/POV
The most-viewed AI videos we studied were the wrong ones for consultants to copy.

In our sample of 25 YouTube videos from 10 channels, the biggest jumps over a channel's normal
views (6 to 10 times) came from "I tested this new tool" and fast news reactions.

Those channels are built for other builders. Builders watch, comment, and don't hire consultants.

The videos that attracted buyers looked quieter: a case study of one firm, one job function done
differently, a teardown of a real process.

If you sell AI advisory, reach from the wrong audience is a cost. It trains the algorithm to show
you to people who will never book a call.

What we rank now, before reach: would a buyer of consulting stop for this?

### LI-B3 · Framework · Expertise · MOF · Methodology/competence
A 3-question check before an AI consultant hits post:

1. Is there a scene? One moment from real work, the client unnamed. If the post could have been
written without ever doing the work, it reads that way.
2. Is there a number the reader can't argue with? Team size, days, hours saved, a count. One is
enough. Never invent it.
3. Does the ending ask something you would actually want answered? LinkedIn now demotes
engagement bait like "comment YES". A real question from a real position still draws replies.

We built this from the posts that outperformed their authors' usual numbers in our study of 43
posts from AI consultants. Three yeses won't make a post take off on their own. Three nos, and it
will most likely stay flat.

### LI-B4 · Surfaced problem / audit · Expertise · BOF-lite · Demand capture (soft)
Webinar posts are where good AI consultants lose their audience.

In our study, webinar and registration posts from an account with around a million followers drew
between 2 and 28 reactions each.

The pattern held across the sample: promotion posts with no value in them underperform, whatever
the follower count.

What worked instead: the useful part goes in the post, and the invitation goes in a P.S. or the
first comment. The post earns the click; the link doesn't have to.

If your next event matters, write the post as if there were no event. Then add one line at the
end.

Soft CTA (P.S., only once the profile funnel is ready, D-02): "If you want the 3-question check we
use on every draft, comment CHECK and we'll send it." Hold until a real person can reply.

### LI-B5 · Personal ("we") / behind the scenes · Personal · MOF · Implementation/demonstration
Half of what we wanted to read, we couldn't.

When we studied AI consultants' LinkedIn posts, about half the profile pages wouldn't load without
signing in. We don't sign in to scrape, and we don't use other people's accounts, so those
creators simply aren't in the sample.

That changes what the study can claim. It leans towards established advisers. It's directional,
not a law.

We'd rather say that than dress up a small sample as proof. It's the same standard we hold for
any number a client puts in a post: if you can't stand behind it, it doesn't go in.
